Guide

Applying to grad school, step by step

What graduate students wish they'd known as undergrads: when to start, what goes in an application, how funding works, and how to choose. Requirements vary by field and program, so always check each program's own website.

Not sure? That's exactly what a ScholarLink mentor is for. Ask a current PhD student what a normal week looks like, and whether they'd choose it again.

2. Timeline

Most programs starting in the fall have deadlines from December to February of the year before. Professional schools often use rolling or earlier deadlines, so check carefully.

  • Freshman–Sophomore yearExplore. Join a research lab or project, get to know professors, and take courses that interest you.
  • Junior yearDeepen your research or work experience. Start a list of programs. Ask a mentor about your field's norms.
  • Summer before senior yearDraft your statement of purpose and CV. Take any required test (GRE, MCAT, LSAT…). Shortlist 6–10 programs.
  • Early fall (Aug–Sept)Ask recommenders, giving them at least 4–6 weeks. Contact potential advisors if your field expects it. Look for fellowship deadlines.
  • Oct–NovRevise your statement with feedback. Tailor it to each program. Request transcripts and score reports.
  • Dec–FebSubmit applications, ideally a few days early. Send recommenders a gentle reminder and a thank-you.
  • Jan–MarInterviews and visit weekends.
  • Mar–AprCompare offers and decide. Many programs ask for a decision on funded offers by about April 15.

3. Choosing programs

  • Research fit comes first for research degrees. Find 2–3 faculty at each program whose recent papers excite you.
  • Look at outcomes. Where do graduates go? How long do students take to finish? Many departments publish this.
  • Check funding. For PhDs, how many years are guaranteed, and is the stipend livable in that city?
  • Talk to current students. They'll tell you about advising culture, workload, and support honestly.
  • Balance your list across reach, match, and likely programs. Application fees add up, so budget for them.

4. Contacting potential advisors

In many lab sciences and some social sciences, emailing faculty before applying helps. You can ask whether they're taking students and show genuine interest. In other fields it's uncommon. Ask a mentor what's normal in yours. Keep it short and specific:

Subject: Prospective PhD student – interest in [specific topic]

Dear Professor [Name],

I'm a senior at the University of Georgia studying [major]. I'm applying to
[Program] for Fall [year] and was excited by your recent work on [specific
paper or project], especially [one specific point].

In [lab/project], I [one or two sentences on your relevant experience].
I'd love to explore [related question] in graduate school.

Are you planning to take new students for [year]? I've attached my CV.

Thank you for your time,
[Name]

No reply after two weeks? One polite follow-up is fine. Silence isn't a rejection; faculty get a lot of email.

5. What's in an application

  • Statement of purpose: your research or professional goals and why this program (see below).
  • Personal or diversity statement: required by some programs. It covers your background and what you'd bring.
  • CV or resume: research, presentations, relevant jobs, skills, awards. Academic CVs list research first.
  • Two or three recommendation letters, ideally from faculty who've seen your research or advanced coursework.
  • Transcripts: unofficial copies are often fine to apply; official ones come after admission.
  • Test scores: many programs have made the GRE optional or dropped it. Check each one. Professional schools usually require their own test.
  • Writing sample: common in the humanities and social sciences.
  • Application fee: ask about fee waivers. Many programs offer them for financial need or participation in programs like McNair.

6. Writing your statement of purpose

Committees want to know: Can this person do research here, and will they thrive? A common structure:

  1. Hook (short): the question or problem that drives you. Skip "ever since I was a child."
  2. Experience (the core): one or two projects. What you did, what you learned, what you'd do differently. Show you understand research.
  3. Goals: what you want to study next and why.
  4. Fit: specific faculty, resources, or courses at this program and how they connect to your goals.
  5. Close: your career direction.

Tips: follow the page or word limit exactly. Be specific: "built a Python pipeline that cut image processing from 3 hours to 10 minutes" beats "gained technical skills." Tailor the fit paragraph to every program. Get feedback from at least two people, including someone in your field. A ScholarLink volunteer can review it.

7. Recommendation letters

  • Ask 4–6 weeks before the first deadline. Earlier is better. Ask: "Do you feel you know me well enough to write a strong letter?"
  • Send a brag sheet: your CV, draft statement, program list with deadlines, and reminders of specific work you did with them.
  • Waive your right to see the letters. Committees trust confidential letters more.
  • Send a reminder about a week before each deadline, and a thank-you note when you're done. Tell them where you end up!

8. Funding and costs

  • PhD: in many fields, admitted students get a tuition waiver plus a stipend for working as a teaching or research assistant. If a PhD offer has no funding, ask why. That's a meaningful signal.
  • Master's: often self-funded. Ask about assistantships, scholarships, and employer tuition support.
  • External fellowships bring prestige and independence. Search for national and field-specific fellowships early, since some deadlines fall in the fall before you apply to programs. Eligibility and availability change from year to year, so check each one's current rules.
  • Hidden costs: application fees, test fees, score reports, and travel. Ask about waivers at every step.

9. Interviews and visits

  • Be ready to explain your past research clearly in 2–3 minutes, including what you did.
  • Prepare questions about mentoring style, lab culture, funding, and what students do after graduating.
  • Talk to current students away from faculty. Ask what they'd change.
  • Send brief thank-you emails afterward.

10. Deciding between offers

  • Compare stipends against cost of living, not just the dollar amount.
  • Confirm how many years of funding are guaranteed, and whether health insurance and fees are covered.
  • Weigh advisor fit and lab culture heavily. They shape your day-to-day life for years.
  • Many programs follow an April 15 deadline for accepting funded offers. Check your offer letters, and decline offers you won't take promptly so others can be admitted.

12. Questions to ask your ScholarLink mentor

  • How did you decide on grad school, and on this program?
  • What does a typical week look like for you?
  • How did you get your first research experience?
  • What made an application stand out in your field?
  • Could you look at my statement or CV, or point me to someone who could?
  • What would you do differently if you were applying again?
  • Who else should I talk to?

13. Where to get more help

  • ScholarLink: request application help or get a mentor.
  • Your faculty advisor and professors know your field's norms and often know faculty elsewhere.
  • UGA Career Center: graduate school advising, resume reviews, and mock interviews.
  • UGA Writing Center: feedback on statements at any stage.
  • CURO (Center for Undergraduate Research Opportunities): ways to get research experience.
  • Each program's website and graduate coordinator are the final word on requirements and deadlines.
