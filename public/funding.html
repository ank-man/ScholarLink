    Funding 101

    How graduate school actually gets paid for, in plain language.

    🏛️

    Funded PhDs are the norm

    In most research PhD programs, especially in the sciences, admitted students get a tuition waiver and a stipend, usually through an assistantship. If a PhD program asks you to pay full tuition yourself, ask why before accepting.

    🧑‍🏫

    Assistantships

    You teach (TA) or do research (RA) about 20 hours a week. In return you get a stipend, tuition waiver, and often help with health insurance. This is how most grad students, including international students, are funded.

    🏅

    Fellowships

    Money with no work duties, so you can focus on research. Competitive and prestigious. Many outside fellowships (NSF, Hertz) go with you to any school.

    🔬

    Research grants

    Smaller awards for a specific cost: fieldwork, travel, equipment, or a summer at a national lab. Great for building your CV.

    🎓

    Master's & professional

    Usually self-funded through savings, loans, and scholarships, though some master's programs offer assistantships. Ask every program what share of students receive aid.

    Tips that make a difference

    • 📅  Start 12 months ahead. Most big fellowships close in the fall, a year before you'd start (the NSF GRFP closes in October).
    • 🏫  Watch for campus deadlines. Rhodes, Marshall, Churchill, and Fulbright need UGA's endorsement first, with an earlier internal deadline. Talk to UGA's major fellowships advisors early.
    • 🔁  Apply more than once. You can often apply as a senior and again in early grad school. Reviewers' feedback makes the second try stronger.
    • 🧾  Stipends are taxable income in the US. Set money aside, because fellowships often don't withhold taxes.
    • 🤝  Ask a mentor to read your essays. A grad student who won the same fellowship is the best reviewer you can get. Request help.
    • 📄  Get your CV ready. Almost every application asks for one. Build it with the CV maker.

    🌍 Guide for international students

    Funding works differently when you're not a US citizen or permanent resident. Here's what to know.

    Where your funding will come from

    • Your program, first. Teaching and research assistantships are open to international students and are the main source of PhD funding. Ask each program: "Do admitted international PhD students receive the same funding package?"
    • Your advisor's grants. Research assistantships are often paid from faculty grants. Contacting faculty before you apply helps (see our cold email guide).
    • Programs from your home country. Fulbright Foreign Student, government scholarships, and national foundations often fund study in the US.
    • Outside awards open to everyone. Knight-Hennessy, Gates Cambridge, AAUW International, P.E.O., Wenner-Gren, and others below.

    What to watch out for

    • Many US fellowships require citizenship or permanent residency, including NSF GRFP, NDSEG, NIH F31, Hertz, and DOE CSGF. Don't spend time on them unless your status changes.
    • Proof of funding: to get your I-20, you'll need to show you can cover the first year. An assistantship offer letter usually counts.
    • Work limits: on an F-1 visa, on-campus work is generally limited to 20 hours a week during the semester. Off-campus work needs authorization (CPT or OPT) first.
    • Taxes: stipends are usually taxable. Your home country may have a tax treaty with the US, and you must file Form 8843 every year, even with no income.
    • Health insurance is required. Check what your assistantship covers.

    Get help at UGA

    UGA's Office of Global Engagement handles international student immigration questions: I-20s, work authorization, and travel. Your graduate coordinator knows your program's funding. A ScholarLink mentor who was an international applicant can share what worked for them; find a mentor.

    This guide is general information, not immigration or tax advice. Rules change, so confirm anything about your visa with the Office of Global Engagement.
